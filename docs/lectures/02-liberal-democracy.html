Why should unelected judges have the last word?

  • this week:

    • why anyone would empower a court

    • the problem this creates: who governs?

    • the concept doing most of the work, judicial independence

  • what does it take for a court to constrain a government that could simply ignore it?

Why courts get power

Why would states establish courts?

  • someone needs to adjudicate disputes

  • this could be the ruler, or a person appointed by the executive

    • but other actors would not perceive the ruler as an impartial adjudicator (Shapiro 1981)

  • committing to an independent court instead makes adjudication more credible

    • and more attractive to other actors

Credible commitments

  • an independent court is a signal: the executive pledges to abide by its decisions

  • credible commitments benefit the government

    • the English crown could borrow more cheaply after the Glorious Revolution (North and Weingast 1989)

    • secure property rights help the economy develop

  • internationally, treaties and courts work in a similar way

    • but also lock in their own, other governments’ and future governments’ preferences (Moravcsik 2000)

Who governs?

Who ultimately governs?

  • empowering courts raises the question of who ultimately governs

  • if governments must abide by what courts say, and courts have the last word, aren’t courts the real rulers?

  • for Hobbes, whoever has the last word is the sovereign

    • setting a judge above the sovereign “is to make a new sovereign” (Hobbes 1651, ch. 29)

How do liberal democracies manage the tension?

  • independent courts on one side, representative government enacting the will of the people on the other

  • liberal democracies manage the tension through constitutionalism and the separation of powers

  • quite successful at maintaining the illusion that everyone is subject to the law (the rule of law)

  • yet subjecting legislative to judicial power is perhaps particularly normatively objectionable (Waldron 2006, 2021)

What is the rule of law?

  • thin (formal) conceptions: law must be general, public, prospective, clear, consistent, possible to comply with, stable and actually applied, the eight desiderata of Fuller (1964)

    • says nothing about the content of the law; an unjust law can satisfy all eight

  • thick (substantive) conceptions add rights, democracy or social justice

  • courts feature in both: as the guarantee of consistent application (thin) and as the guardians of rights (thick)

Separation of powers and judicial review

Separation of powers

  • separation of powers: legislative, executive and judicial functions sit with separate branches, staffed by different people, each confined to its own function (Vile 1967)

  • there is no liberty if the power of judging is not separated from legislative and executive power (Montesquieu 1748)

  • in practice, checks and balances: the branches overlap so that each can restrain the others

Separation of powers

  • even in systems where judicial independence is strong, there are many formal points of contact between the judiciary, the legislature and the executive

    • appointment of judges

    • competence disputes

    • judicial review of administrative acts

    • judicial review of statutory law

Designs of constitutional review

  • who reviews: one constitutional court (centralized) or every court (diffuse)

    • centralized: Austria, Germany, Italy, most of post-1989 Europe; diffuse: US, Argentina, Japan, the Nordic countries

  • when: before the law takes effect (ex ante or a priori, France only until 2010) or after (ex post or a posteriori, almost everywhere else)

  • how: in the abstract, detached from any case (usually referral by politicians), or concretely, in a dispute between parties

  • who can trigger it: politicians, courts, ombudsmen, individuals (directly challenging a law in Germany or Colombia)

  • how strong: strike down (strong form) or leave parliament the last word (weak form)

    • weak form: declaration of incompatibility (UK Human Rights Act 1998, New Zealand) or legislative override (Canada)

  • each dimension changes which cases arrive and who can be blamed for the result

Ex ante v ex post review

  • one type of indirect effect of judicialization is legislator moral hazard (Fox and Stephenson 2011; Ward and Gabel 2019)

    • knowledge that courts will correct ill-advised policies makes legislators behave more recklessly

  • this type of moral hazard is more common with ex ante judicial review (Ward and Gabel 2019)

French Constitutional Council is the archetype of the ex ante review effect on legislative recklessness

legislators promising unconstitutional policies can make it harder for voters to make good decisions

The counter-majoritarian difficulty

  • Bickel (1962): an unelected court striking down the acts of elected majorities

Judicial power and independence

Who cares about courts?

  • it is commonly assumed that court rulings must be complied with and followed by affected actors in virtually all constitutional systems

  • but what are the sources of this judicial power? why and when does anyone listen to courts?

Concept of judicial power

  • following Staton and Moore (2011), we can usefully identify two pillars of judicial power:

    • autonomy – or independence in a strict sense – or the extent to which judges’ decisions reflect their true preferences as opposed to someone else’s

    • effectiveness – or compliance – or the extent to which courts can compel actors to comply with adverse decisions

How can courts increase their power?

  • new courts consolidate power by not antagonizing the government for an initial period

  • expand jurisdiction through broad interpretation of access rules and legal domains

  • a common strategy is to first create the power-expanding principle without applying it in the initial case to mitigate costs of compliance (Marbury)

What makes judges autonomous?

  • judicial independence is the single most important attribute associated with modern judiciaries

  • yet there is disagreement on the precise content of this concept

  • independence to do what? independence from what or whom?

Judicial independence

the degree to which judges actually decide cases in accordance with their own determinations of the evidence, the law and justice, free from the coercion, blandishments, interference, or threats from governmental authorities or private citizens. (Rosenn 1987, 8)

What is(n’t) judicial independence

  • can a judge be independent when the judiciary as a whole isn’t?

  • does “dependence” on another judge’s or court’s decision undermine judicial independence?

What is(n’t) judicial independence

  • what about anticipatory judicial behaviour?

    • a court may self-censor if it knows the government is likely to override its decision

  • free from “governmental authorities or private citizens”

  • rulings based on “own determinations of … the law” vs “the law”

Measuring judicial independence

  • there are three main ways of measuring judicial independence

    • de jure measures code formal guarantees such as tenure and removal rules, so they are easy to compare but may not reflect practice

    • de facto measures rely on expert surveys (V-Dem), human rights reports (CIRI) or court rulings, so they are closer to practice but more subjective

    • latent measures combine many imperfect indicators into a single estimate (Linzer and Staton 2015)

  • different measures often disagree with each other (Ríos-Figueroa and Staton 2014)

  • court rulings are a misleading indicator

    • a court that never rules against the government may be captured, or it may never have been asked to

    • a government that expects to lose in court may not legislate in the first place
