Appointing and Monitoring Judges

Week 6

Dr Michal Ovádek

Controlling the judiciary

  • how do politicians keep tabs on judges?

  • how do they ensure that delegation of judicial power does not go beyond what they originally envisaged?

  • how do judges govern themselves?

Ex ante and ex post control

  • we can generally divide tools of political control in two categories (Helfer and Slaughter 2005)

    • ex ante: mechanisms of control designed prior to the court’s operation

    • ex post: mechanisms of control developed in response to the court’s operation

Jurisdiction

  • a key decision liable to influence how much and over what types of issues a court has power concerns jurisdiction (or access / standing / locus standi)

  • jurisdiction is about regulating access to courts

    • in general, the more actors can access a court, the greater its power

  • there are multiple ways of defining (= constraining) a court’s jurisdiction and usually more than one is used at once

    • geographical (e.g. US Courts of Appeals)

    • substantive (e.g. UK Supreme Court re Scottish criminal law; higher courts ruling on “points of law only”)

    • personal (e.g. CJEU rules on standing)

Jurisdiction (personal)

  • the CJEU has famously restrictive access rules for private litigants (Art. 263(4) TFEU)

Any natural or legal person may […] institute proceedings against an act addressed to that person or which is of direct and individual concern to them, and against a regulatory act which is of direct concern to them and does not entail implementing measures.

Who becomes a judge?

Who becomes a judge?

  • if judges were randomly sampled from a country’s population, their preferences would be on average (in expectation) similar to the population’s

    • there would also be a lot of heterogeneity (as much as in the population)

  • in reality, judicial preferences are systematically influenced by a set of institutional factors

Selection effects

  • what needed to happen for someone to become a judge on court X?

    • what kind of person wants to become a legal professional? (internal)

    • what kind of person is likely to be appointed a judge? (external)

  • answers to these questions will be shaped by institutional features

Gender variation on the bench

  • US appellate judges’ gender influences their votes in sex discrimination cases (Boyd, Epstein, and Martin 2010)

  • the proportion of female judges varies greatly between countries

    • between a third and a half in common law countries

    • over 70% in some post-socialist countries (Havelková, Kosař, and Urbániková 2022)

  • reforming the selection process speeds up the appointment of the first woman to a peak court (Arrington et al. 2021)

Women in the Czech judiciary

  • Havelková, Kosař, and Urbániková (2022) illustrate the impact of institutional factors on gender representation in the Czech judiciary

  • low pay and prestige under socialism drew many women to the bench

    • many male judges left for private practice after 1989 and low trainee pay still deters men

    • judging is easier to combine with family life than other legal jobs, which attracts women

  • despite higher overall numbers (61% of judges), the most important positions are occupied by men

    • the childcare penalty hampers career progression but female judges largely accept their situation as given (Urbániková, Havelková, and Kosař 2024)

Who ends up on the bench?

  • class and education also shape who becomes a judge

  • 62% of senior UK judges went to private school, compared with 7% of the population (Sutton Trust 2025)

    • 75% of them went to Oxford or Cambridge

    • these shares have barely changed since 2019, partly because the bench turns over slowly

  • both selection effects are at work, from who can afford bar training to who is asked to apply

Appointments

Appointment procedures

  • the most salient ex ante mechanism

  • the designers of international courts trade off judicial independence, accountability and transparency (Dunoff and Pollack 2017)

  • there is considerable variety in how judges are appointed around the world

Variation in appointment procedures

Constitutional and supreme courts, based on Tiede (2024)

How can judges be chosen?

Appointment procedures

  • mixed systems encourage a tendency for each appointing branch to select judges most closely aligned with its interests

    • this cleavage may supersede partisan preferences (Tiede 2020, 2024)

  • mixed systems can hinder the functioning of collegial courts

    • but judges with different selectors bring distinct voices, which can enhance deliberation (Tiede 2020)

  • more veto players => more moderate judges

Judicial councils

  • institutional vehicle for judicial self-governance

  • in charge of appointing judges, promotions, discipline and sometimes budgets

  • strongly promoted by international organizations in the 2000s (Bobek and Kosař 2014)

  • trade-off between insulation and external accountability (Garoupa and Ginsburg 2009)

talk about composition:

  • are judges dominant? peak or ordinary?

  • who else appoints members?

Appointing international judges

  • most international judges are nominated by governments and then elected by an assembly

    • ECtHR: each state sends three names, the Parliamentary Assembly of the Council of Europe elects one

    • ICJ: elected by the UN General Assembly and the Security Council from national-group nominations

    • CJEU: governments appoint by common accord after consulting the Article 255 panel

Appointing international judges

  • the Article 255 panel found about one in five first-time candidates unsuitable between 2010 and 2026 (Article 255 Panel 2026)

    • governments followed every opinion between 2010 and 2022 (Article 255 Panel 2022)

    • no candidate for renewal received a negative opinion

  • aspiring EU members and pro-integration governments appoint more activist ECtHR judges (Voeten 2007)

  • the politics of selection across international courts is reviewed by Larsson et al. (2023)

Confirmation wars

  • US senators are more likely to vote for a Supreme Court nominee who is popular in their state (Kastellec, Lax, and Phillips 2010)

  • when the branches are divided, the appointment power becomes the power not to appoint

    • the Senate left Merrick Garland’s 2016 nomination without a hearing for over nine months, then confirmed Amy Coney Barrett within a month in 2020

  • shared appointment power can moderate judges, but sharers who wait each other out leave long vacancies

Tenure and monitoring

Tenure

  • the tenure of judges can differ markedly by country and type of court

    • most ordinary judges are appointed for life, sometimes with age limits

    • judges on constitutional and international courts are often term-bound and can sometimes be reappointed

UK Supreme Court has mandatory retirement at 75

ICJ judges have 9-year renewable terms

ECtHR judges had their tenures increased from 6 to 9 but made non-renewable

Reappointment

  • the prospect of reappointment or re-election influences judicial behaviour

    • judges who are up for reappointment are more responsive to the preferences of their principal

    • US state supreme court justices facing re-election make more popular decisions than in their final term (Hall 2014)

    • ending renewable terms at the ECtHR reduced judges’ tendency to favour their nominating state (Stiansen 2022)

    • secret voting at the CJEU does not shield judges from ideological deselection by governments (Hermansen and Naurin 2026)

  • non-renewable terms can however damage a court’s effectiveness

    • turnover can affect retention of expertise

Transparency

  • the principals’ ability to monitor (and therefore punish/reward) judicial behaviour is conditional on transparency

  • while decisions are always public, individual votes and dissents often are not

  • do principals have other means of obtaining information about the voting of judges?

Renewal and transparency

The monitoring toolkit

tool who uses it what it reveals or punishes
reappointment and promotion governments, court presidents, councils rewards deference (Ramseyer and Rasmusen 2001)
transparency of votes and dissents everyone who voted how; dissents at the ECtHR, none at the CJEU
performance evaluation and case statistics councils, ministries speed and output, not direction
disciplinary regimes councils, special chambers misconduct and sometimes the content of rulings
budgets and staffing legislatures, ministries capacity; hits the whole institution

Judicial exits

  • in established democracies judges are rarely forced out of office through removal procedures

    • elsewhere politicians use removal procedures and informal pressure to purge courts (Pérez-Liñán and Castagnola 2024)

  • the motivations behind a judge quitting can be more varied however

Strategic retirements

  • hypothesis: federal judges in the US prefer to retire under presidents of the same political party that first appointed them (Stolzenberg and Lindgren 2022; Nixon and Haskin 2000)

    • Stolzenberg and Lindgren (2022) argue that this behaviour is driven more by reciprocity norms (giving back to appointing party) than ideology

    • in contrast, Deschler and Sen (2024) find that moderate Republican appointees appear to wait out conservative presidents such as Trump (ideology matters, not just party)

  • US district judges weigh their ideological fit with the president only once eligible for a pension (Hansford, Savchak, and Songer 2010)

  • understudied outside the US, and the theory finds limited support across the Americas (Pérez-Liñán and Araya 2017)

Elections

Judicial elections

  • perceived rejection of “elite-driven” judicial selection

  • tried out in revolutionary France (1790) and Central America post Spanish independence (Driscoll and Nelson 2015)

  • 38 US states hold elections, including retention elections, for their supreme courts (Brusgard 2025)

    • elected and appointed state judges behave differently in sentencing and discrimination cases (Lim 2013; Lim, Snyder Jr., and Strömberg 2015; Besley and Payne 2013)

    • the 2025 Wisconsin Supreme Court election saw over 100 million USD in campaign spending (Bauer 2025)

    • Kansas voters rejected direct elections for supreme court justices in August 2026 (Kansas Secretary of State 2026)

the 2025 Wisconsin figure was roughly double the previous record of 51 million USD, set in Wisconsin in 2023. Wisconsin has a population of less than 6 million (cf UK almost 70)

Electing judges outside the US: Bolivia

  • Driscoll and Nelson (2015) evaluate Bolivia introducing judicial elections in 2011

    • judges became much more diverse as a result (in contrast to the US)

    • confidence in the judiciary increased among government supporters but declined overall

  • in both 2011 and 2017 more than half of the ballots cast were blank or spoilt

  • the 2024 election came a year late after disputes over candidate screening and was only partial (International IDEA 2024)

Electing judges outside the US: Mexico

  • 1 June 2025: the first of two rounds electing the whole federal judiciary under the 2024 reform

    • all nine Supreme Court seats, part of the electoral tribunal, a new disciplinary tribunal and hundreds of federal judgeships

    • candidates pre-screened by evaluation committees of the three branches; no party labels on the ballot

  • turnout was only around 13 percent of eligible voters (Al Jazeera 2025)

Electing judges outside the US: Mexico

  • the winners overwhelmingly aligned with the governing party Morena

  • a 2026 reform moved the election of the remaining posts from 2027 to 2028 (Presidencia de la República 2026)

  • the stated aims were to democratize an elitist judiciary and end corruption

    • the result is a judiciary chosen under one party’s dominance

Reforms as natural experiments

  • when appointment or tenure rules change, the change is a shock we can learn from

  • difference-in-differences: compare courts exposed to the reform with those not exposed, before and after

    • the untouched group supplies the counterfactual trend

  • Bolivia’s 2011 reform left no untouched comparison group, so Driscoll and Nelson (2015) compare surveys before and after

  • this requires that nothing else changed at the same time for the treated group only
