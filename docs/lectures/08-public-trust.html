How the Public Perceives Courts

Week 8

Dr Michal Ovádek

Legitimacy and public support

  • what are the determinants of public support of courts?

  • to what extent are courts immune from changes in public opinion?

  • this week: why and how public support matters for courts, what it is made of, how courts cultivate it and how we measure it

Why support matters for courts

Public support as a source of judicial power

  • one reason why courts’ autonomy and effectiveness can be high is public support (Carrubba and Zorn 2010)

  • in general, people hold courts in high regard (Georg Vanberg 2015)

    • this is helped by the fact that courts are often responsive to public preferences (Mishler and Sheehan 1993)

  • attacking courts and non-compliance is usually politically costly

Trust across institutions

  • across 30 OECD countries in late 2023, 54% reported high or moderately high trust in the courts and legal system, against 39% for the national government (OECD Trust Survey 2024)

    • Denmark: 75% for the courts, 44% for the government

  • the same ordering holds in Britain: in a 2026 British Election Study sample, the Supreme Court was the most trusted of six institutions and political parties the least (Ovádek and Perrett 2026)

  • courts are trusted more than parliaments and governments in almost every survey

Is trust in courts its own thing?

  • trust in parliaments and in courts moves together; two readings

    • elite contagion: one governing establishment, so distrust of one branch spreads to the others

    • institutional substitution: checks and balances, so distrust of one branch raises trust in the branch that constrains it

  • a British Election Study experiment on Parliament, the UK Supreme Court and the ECtHR (Ovádek and Perrett 2026)

    • an unpopular parliamentary decision lowered trust in the courts more than in Parliament

    • an unpopular ruling lowered trust in that court and others, not in Parliament

    • popular decisions, party cues and information on judges’ conduct did not move trust

  • the reservoir of goodwill can be drained by decisions the courts never made

Support as a shield

  • public support makes enforcement more likely and makes legislators wary of open defiance (G. Vanberg 2001)

  • does support deter court curbing?

    • in experiments, US voters punish incumbents who attack courts, especially those who esteem the court (Driscoll and Nelson 2023)

    • co-partisans punish less, and party and issue positions matter more

    • but the public supports curbing when it disagrees with the court (Bartels and Johnston 2020), and populist voters in Germany back limiting the constitutional court’s power (Peffley and Rohrschneider 2024)

    • the shield holds only where the public values the institution over the outcome

Courts track public opinion

  • if support is a resource, courts should protect it; the evidence that they do:

    • the US Supreme Court’s decisions move with public mood over time (Mishler and Sheehan 1993; Casillas, Enns, and Wohlfarth 2011)

    • when courts fear non-implementation, they side with majorities – “judicial majoritarianism” (Hall and Ura 2015)

    • unpopular courts behave differently when the public is paying attention (Krehbiel 2021)

  • a court that tracks opinion keeps its support and gives up the counter-majoritarian role that justified it

Maintaining public support

  • to maintain diffuse support, it is arguably in courts’ long-term interests to stay ideologically close to the median voter

  • courts can offer compensation to the “losers” to increase acceptance (Gonzalez-Ocantos and Dinas 2019)

  • courts might care less about maintaining public support when

    • they are aligned with the executive (for enforcement)

    • the public is not paying attention (but see Casillas, Enns, and Wohlfarth (2011))

    • public opinion is less important (autocracies)

What support is made of

Legitimacy

Legitimacy is a psychological property of an authority, institution, or social arrangement that leads those connected to it to believe that it is appropriate, proper, and just. (Tyler 2006)

  • sometimes used interchangeably with “authority” and “diffuse support”

    • conceptually different from “specific support” (but measurement difficulties)

Judicial legitimacy

  • unlike democratic parliaments and executives, most courts cannot derive their legitimacy from electoral mandates

  • at the same time, courts rely on diffuse support for effectiveness more than other branches

  • particularly important when constituents disagree over a policy decision

Sources of judicial legitimacy

  • traditionally, legalism and legal symbols are seen as the main source of legitimacy (Caldeira and Gibson 1992) – positivity theory (Gibson and Nelson 2017)

    • symbols make courts seem fairer and different from politics

    • awareness means more exposure to symbols and a “repository of goodwill”

    • legalistic framing of a ruling induces specific support (Magalhães et al. 2023)

  • the ideological rival: people ascribe less legitimacy to a court they think they disagree with (Bartels and Johnston 2013)

Where does support come from?

Political foundations

  • we would expect that individuals holding democratic values are more supportive of judicial independence

    • Bartels, Horowitz, and Kramon (2023) find no evidence of this in the context of 2017 Kenyan elections

    • Cheruvu, Krehbiel, and Mussell (2025) find less support for anti-court backlash among more democratically minded Hungarians

  • a study of 34 African countries shows that presidential co-partisanship decreases support for horizontal judicial power and increases it for vertical (Bartels and Kramon 2020)

    • support for courts depends on who is in power

Political context

  • in line with positivity theory, greater awareness of a court should be associated with more support for its independence (Gibson, Lodge, and Woodson 2014)

  • Gandur, Chewning, and Driscoll (2025) show that this relationship is affected by the political context

    • in Poland and Hungary, the perception of executive influence on courts creates even more demand for judicial independence than in US and Germany, but awareness alone does not

    • De Micheli and Taylor (2024) similarly show that knowledge alone of Latin American courts does not increase trust

  • identification with anti-system parties may reduce trust in courts (Arzheimer 2024), and individual-level illiberal views about justice are negatively correlated with trust in the EU, the ECtHR and domestic judiciaries (Voeten 2022)

Perceptions of judicial fairness

  • judges’ identity categories operate as ideological cues affecting perceptions of judicial fairness (Bracic et al. 2023)

    • marginalized identities increase Democrats’ perceptions of fairness, impartiality and trust in SCOTUS

    • Republicans see gay judges as less fair and impartial than straight judges

  • descriptive representation seems to matter little

    • people prefer judges who (they assume) will think like them, not look like them

Descriptive representation and trust

  • does a bench that looks like the public earn more trust from it?

  • Black respondents rated the US federal courts as more legitimate when told about Black judges on the bench, white respondents less so (Scherer and Curry 2010)

  • whether the same holds for women on the bench is the question tested by Moyer (2025)

  • representation adds legitimacy with the group represented and can cost it with others

International courts and public opinion

  • support for international courts is context dependent

    • which court, which country, which narratives (Zvobgo and Chaudoin 2025)

  • but generally tends to be correlated with trust in national courts and adjacent international organizations at the individual level (Stappert and Verhaegen 2025)

Zvobgo and Chaudoin (2025) do not find evidence that arguments about ICC’s complementarity are convincing to global public

Measuring legitimacy

  • diffuse support (legitimacy): loyalty to the institution regardless of what it does; specific support: approval of its recent decisions

  • the standard battery asks whether the court should be abolished or its jurisdiction reduced if it kept making unpopular decisions, whether it can be trusted to decide in the country’s interest, whether it gets too mixed up in politics

  • most cross-national surveys have a single item, “trust in the courts”, which mixes the two and tracks the news cycle

  • the battery itself is under fire: it may measure attitudes to the court’s recent output as much as loyalty (Hemrajani 2026)

  • cross-national comparison needs the same questions asked at the same time; smoothing across surveys is one workaround (Claassen 2019)

Trust in courts, over time and across countries

  • https://trustincourts.netlify.app/: an annual trust estimate for each country, with its uncertainty

  • built by harmonizing surveys that ask different questions on different scales in different years, using a measurement model

  • compare countries, follow one country over time, and set trust in courts against trust in the national parliament

  • a starting point for a research paper on why trust differs between countries or moves within one

How courts reach the public

How courts reach the public

  • what the public knows of courts arrives through the media, and courts manage that

  • constitutional courts issue press releases selectively, more for salient and politically sensitive rulings (Meyer 2020)

  • the Mexican Supreme Court began promoting its rulings to the press to build the support it lacked, choosing which decisions to publicize (Staton 2006)

  • the UK Supreme Court streams its hearings and hands down judgments on camera; the US Supreme Court still refuses cameras

  • being elected adds to a court’s legitimacy, while campaign contributions and attack advertising reduce it (Gibson 2012)

  • majorities in most countries cannot name a judge of their highest court

Rulings that land mid-survey

Rulings that land mid-survey

  • surveys take weeks to field; if a ruling drops in the middle, respondents interviewed before and after can be compared as if randomly assigned to “before” and “after” – the unexpected event during survey design (Muñoz, Falcó-Gimeno, and Hernández 2020)

  • the assumptions: the event was not anticipated, who gets interviewed on which day is unrelated to it, and nothing else happened at the same time

  • applied to Argentine rulings by Gandur (2025)

  • checks for any such study

    • are the before and after samples alike?

    • does the effect fade within days?

    • could the timing of interviews have shifted because of the event?
